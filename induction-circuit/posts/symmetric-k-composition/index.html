1 Why positive eigenvalues suggest matching

1.1 The joint matrix of a writer and a reader

In the induction pattern [A][B]…[A]→[B][A][B]\ldots[A]\to[B], two attention heads work together. At the historical BB position, an earlier head—the writer ww—uses its QK circuit to attend to the preceding AA. Its value projection VwV_w reads the residual stream at AA, and its output projection OwO_w writes the resulting information into the residual stream at BB. The later reader applies KrK_r to this updated residual stream to form its key at historical BB, containing information about the preceding AA.

At the current AA position, the reader rr uses QrQ_r to form a query from the current residual stream. Its QK circuit compares this query with the keys at historical positions. For induction, this comparison favors the historical BB position because its key carries information about an AA matching the current token. The reader’s OV circuit then reads information about the historical successor BB from its residual stream and writes it into the residual stream at the current position, supporting the prediction of BB.

Using column vectors, define their K-composition matrix and its bilinear score by Cr,w=Qr⊤KrOwVw,sC(x,y)=x⊤Cr,wy.C_{r,w}=Q_r^\top K_rO_wV_w,\qquad s_C(x,y)=x^\top C_{r,w}y.

(1)

Here x,y∈ℝdx,y\in\mathbb{R}^d are reader and writer inputs: xx represents current AA, while yy can represent any historical source token, matching or competing. The projection dimensions are Qr,Kr∈ℝhr×dQ_r,K_r\in\mathbb{R}^{h_r\times d}, Vw∈ℝhw×dV_w\in\mathbb{R}^{h_w\times d}, and Ow∈ℝd×hwO_w\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h_w}. The source contributes awKrOwVwya_wK_rO_wV_wy to the reader’s key at a candidate position, with writer attention weight awa_w. A previous-token writer typically gives the preceding token most weight. We omit awa_w and analyze x⊤Cr,wyx^\top C_{r,w}y.

A central question here is how this composition assigns a higher attention score to xx and yy representing the same token identity than to inputs representing different tokens, so that the reader can attend to and read from the correct historical successor.

1.2 A spectral clue, rather than a ranking conclusion

In 2022, Anthropic associated positive eigenvalues of the token-space matrix in a pure K-composition term with “same-matching” [2]. For one reader–writer pair, that matrix is Wr,w=E⊤Cr,wEW_{r,w}=E^\top C_{r,w}E, where EE maps one-hot token vectors to embeddings. Both contain the same QK–OV product, but Wr,wW_{r,w} includes embeddings and acts on one-hot inputs, while Cr,wC_{r,w} acts on residual representations. For the same one-hot vector tt on both sides, t⊤Wr,wt=(Et)⊤Cr,w(Et)t^\top W_{r,w}t=(Et)^\top C_{r,w}(Et).

Anthropic weighted these matrices by pwp_w, the earlier head’s mean attention to the immediately preceding position in example text, and computed the trace of their sum: W̃r=∑w:ℓw<ℓrpwWr,w,tr⁡(W̃r)=∑jλj(W̃r),\widetilde W_r=\sum_{w:\,\ell_w<\ell_r}p_w W_{r,w},\qquad \operatorname{tr}(\widetilde W_r)=\sum_j\lambda_j(\widetilde W_r), where ℓw\ell_w and ℓr\ell_r denote the heads’ layers. Anthropic reported a correlation with behavioral prefix-matching scores in their small models [2].

The distinction between token embeddings and deep residual-stream representations matters here. Anthropic’s statistic selects pure K-composition paths: the query is projected directly from the embedding, while the key-side path passes the embedding through the writer’s OV matrix and then the reader’s K projection [2]. Deep residual inputs, however, contain not just token embeddings but also contributions from earlier attention heads and, when present, MLPs. We therefore use x⊤Cr,wyx^\top C_{r,w}y to study matching between these contextual representations without restricting them to embeddings.

1.3 Select functional Writer–Reader routes

At a historical token position, the residual stream contains OV outputs written by many attention heads in earlier layers. The Reader forms its key from this combined representation. We focus on which of these OV messages actually supports the Reader’s induction behavior: attending from the current token A to a historical B that followed an earlier A. We test candidate heads individually and call the behaviorally relevant sources Writers.

Construct the repeated input. We use 5,000 medium-frequency token identities, defined by empirical frequency ranks 5,001–10,000 among 50,252 eligible shared token identities in a fixed Pythia reference corpus. This excludes the 5,000 most frequent eligible tokens and the lower-frequency tail. “Medium-frequency” refers to this reference ranking, rather than a universal boundary between common and rare tokens. Tokens here can be word pieces, not necessarily complete words.

We randomly group and order tokens from this pool into 25-token blocks, with no repeated token identity inside a block. The blocks are random token sequences, not natural-language passages. Each block is copied four times after the beginning-of-sequence token (BOS), producing a 101-token input. The assignment is balanced so that each of the 5,000 identities is a designated query token in ten distinct random blocks.

A query is one position where we inspect the Reader’s attention. We evaluate designated positions in copies 2, 3, and 4 of the block: each then has an earlier occurrence whose successor can be retrieved. Copy 1 provides the initial history and is not evaluated. This is what “the final three occurrences” means. There are therefore

5,000token identities×10contexts per identity×3evaluated copies=150,000query positions.5{,}000\ \text{token identities}\times10\ \text{contexts per identity}\times3\ \text{evaluated copies}=150{,}000\ \text{query positions}.

A normal block has 24 designated query tokens; its 25th token supplies the successor of the 24th. We pack the 50,000 token–context combinations into 2,083 such blocks and one final block with eight designated tokens: 2,083×24+8=50,0002{,}083\times24+8=50{,}000. This gives 2,084 input sequences. The remaining positions in the last block are fillers. Every block, including its fillers, is repeated unchanged four times.

Measure attention to the correct successors. At a query A, the Reader assigns attention probabilities to the positions it can see. Softmax converts its raw attention scores, called logits, into probabilities that sum to one. For induction, the correct locations are the historical B positions immediately after earlier occurrences of A. We add the attention probabilities assigned to all these locations. For example, probabilities of 0.20, 0.15, and 0.10 on three correct B positions give a total of 0.45: 45% of the Reader’s attention goes to correct successors.

Let 𝒫t\mathcal P_t denote these correct historical positions for query tt, and let ℓr(t,⋅)\ell_r(t,\cdot) denote the Reader’s original-model attention logits. The total correct-successor attention is

ar(t)=∑p∈𝒫tsoftmax⁡(ℓr(t,⋅))p.a_r(t)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_t}\operatorname{softmax}(\ell_r(t,\cdot))_p.

Combine the three evaluated copies. The same designated token is tested once in copy 2, once in copy 3, and once in copy 4 of each input sequence. We calculate these three results separately because their available histories differ:

For token identity uu in context cc, write ar,u,c(k)a_{r,u,c}^{(k)} for the total correct-successor attention at its query in copy kk. Each result first sums the probabilities on the correct B positions visible to that query. We then take the equal-weight mean of the three results, rather than using only copy 4:

a‾r,u,c=ar,u,c(2)+ar,u,c(3)+ar,u,c(4)3.\bar a_{r,u,c}=\frac{a_{r,u,c}^{(2)}+a_{r,u,c}^{(3)}+a_{r,u,c}^{(4)}}{3}.

Finally, we average equally over the ten contexts of each token and over all 5,000 token identities:

a‾r=15,000∑u=15,000110∑c=110a‾r,u,c.\bar a_r=\frac1{5{,}000}\sum_{u=1}^{5{,}000}\frac1{10}\sum_{c=1}^{10}\bar a_{r,u,c}.

The sum over correct positions is therefore within one query. The mean over copies combines the three tests of the same token in the same context; the outer means combine tokens and contexts. The three copies retain equal weight even though they have one, two, and three correct historical B positions, respectively.

Test each candidate attention head. For each candidate attention head ww in an earlier layer, its QK circuit determines how strongly it attends from a historical position B to the preceding A; its OV circuit writes the corresponding message into B’s residual stream. We isolate this QK-weighted OV contribution from the previous token and subtract its contribution to the Reader’s key. This removes the Reader’s QK matching against that specific message. The Reader’s QK parameters and its matching against the other OV messages remain intact. The actual query and the recorded Reader normalization denominator are also held fixed, and attention probabilities are recomputed over all visible positions.

Write ar\w(t)a_{r\setminus w}(t) for the total attention to correct successors after this removal. The removal decrease is combined in the same order. For each token uu and context cc, we compute the before-minus-after decrease separately in copies 2, 3, and 4, then average those three decreases equally:

Δ‾w→r;u,c=13∑k=24[ar,u,c(k)−ar\w,u,c(k)].\bar\Delta_{w\to r;u,c}=\frac13\sum_{k=2}^{4}\left[a_{r,u,c}^{(k)}-a_{r\setminus w,u,c}^{(k)}\right].

The route’s final Reader score averages these values equally over all tokens and contexts:

Δw→r=15,000∑u=15,000110∑c=110Δ‾w→r;u,c=𝔼t[ar(t)−ar\w(t)].\Delta_{w\to r}=\frac1{5{,}000}\sum_{u=1}^{5{,}000}\frac1{10}\sum_{c=1}^{10}\bar\Delta_{w\to r;u,c}=\mathbb E_t\!\left[a_r(t)-a_{r\setminus w}(t)\right].

Here tt ranges over all query positions in copies 2, 3, and 4, giving 5,000×10×3=150,0005{,}000\times10\times3=150{,}000 tests in total. Thus the expectation over tt includes all three evaluated copies; it is not an average restricted to copy 4.

This is a route-removal attention decrease, not the reader’s unmodified attention probability and not a relative percentage decrease. For example, Δ=0.20\Delta=0.20 means a decrease of 20 percentage points.

At Pythia’s final checkpoint, step 143,000, we retain every route satisfying both

Δw→r>0.10,PrevAttn⁡(w)>0.10.\Delta_{w\to r}>0.10,\qquad \operatorname{PrevAttn}(w)>0.10.

The first condition requires removing the candidate head’s contribution to reduce correct-successor attention by more than ten percentage points. The second requires the head itself to assign more than 10% attention to the previous token on this probe. The retained heads are the Writers for the corresponding Reader routes.

The resulting cohort has 193 routes into 104 distinct Readers, across 70M, 160M, 410M, 1B, 1.4B, and 2.8B models. Their route counts are 10, 29, 36, 18, 36, and 64, respectively. Several routes may share a Reader or a Writer. We track these same endpoint-selected routes backward through the 19 checkpoints; early-checkpoint plots describe eventual endpoint routes, not routes newly selected at each earlier step. Negative early removal effects are retained.

The older OLMo matrix survey is used only as a separately identified descriptive comparison in Section 2.3. It is not presented as another cohort selected by this Pythia route-removal protocol.

2 Decomposing the matrix and its energy

2.1 Positive, negative, and antisymmetric contributions

Every real square matrix has the exact decomposition H=C+C⊤2,A=C−C⊤2,C=H+A.H=\frac{C+C^\top}{2},\qquad A=\frac{C-C^\top}{2},\qquad C=H+A.

(6)

Because A⊤=−AA^\top=-A, the scalar x⊤Axx^\top Ax is equal to its own negative: x⊤Ax=(x⊤Ax)⊤=x⊤A⊤x=−x⊤Ax=0.x^\top Ax=(x^\top Ax)^\top=x^\top A^\top x=-x^\top Ax=0.

(7)

Consequently x⊤Cx=x⊤Hxx^\top Cx=x^\top Hx. This cancellation holds for the same vector on both sides. It does not remove AA from a cross-context score x⊤Cyx^\top Cy with x≠yx\ne y.

The real symmetric matrix HH has a complete orthonormal eigenbasis. Writing H=UΛU⊤H=U\Lambda U^\top separates mutually orthogonal directions, allowing its quadratic form to be read without nonsymmetric cross terms. Define H+=∑k:λk>0λkukuk⊤,H−=∑k:λk<0λkukuk⊤.{H^+}=\sum_{k:\,\lambda_k>0}\lambda_k u_ku_k^\top,\qquad {H^-}=\sum_{k:\,\lambda_k<0}\lambda_k u_ku_k^\top.

Here uku_k is the eigenvector for λk\lambda_k, and C=H++H−+AC={H^+}+{H^-}+A. Writing a=U⊤xa=U^\top x gives x⊤H+x=∑k:λk>0λkak2≥0,x⊤H−x=∑k:λk<0λkak2≤0.x^\top{H^+} x=\sum_{k:\,\lambda_k>0}\lambda_k a_k^2\ge0,\qquad x^\top{H^-} x=\sum_{k:\,\lambda_k<0}\lambda_k a_k^2\le0.

(9)

Every squared coordinate ak2a_k^2 is nonnegative, so the retained eigenvalue signs determine both inequalities. Either score is zero exactly when xx has no component in its corresponding positive or negative eigenspace.

The positive component rewards agreement in its weighted directions.

2.2 An additive energy accounting

Define the matrix energy as its squared Frobenius norm. Symmetric and antisymmetric matrices are orthogonal under the Frobenius inner product. In addition, H+{H^+} and H−{H^-} occupy disjoint eigenspaces, so ⟨H+,H−⟩F=0\langle{H^+},{H^-}\rangle_F=0. Expanding the squared norm therefore gives ∥C∥F2=∥H+∥F2+∥H−∥F2+∥A∥F2.\left\lVert C\right\rVert_F^2=\left\lVert {H^+}\right\rVert_F^2+\left\lVert {H^-}\right\rVert_F^2+\left\lVert A\right\rVert_F^2.

(10)

For each composition path, we calculate its full-distance energy fractions in three steps: first calculate squared energy separately at every distance, then sum those energies over distances, and finally take the component-to-complete-C ratio. At each distance D=0,…,2047D=0,\ldots,2047, we form HD+H_D^+, HD−H_D^-, and ADA_D, retaining every eigenvalue, and calculate their squared Frobenius norms. For any one component BB, the resulting fraction is

fB=∑D=02047∥BD∥F2∑D=02047∥CD∥F2,B∈{H+,H−,A}.f_B=\frac{\sum_{D=0}^{2047}\|B_D\|_F^2}{\sum_{D=0}^{2047}\|C_D\|_F^2},\qquad B\in\{H^+,H^-,A\}.

Equivalently, divide both the numerator and denominator by 2,048. For the positive component, this gives

fH+=12048∑D=02047∥HD+∥F212048∑D=02047∥CD∥F2=mean per-distance squared energy of H+mean per-distance squared energy of C.f_{H^+}=\frac{\frac1{2048}\sum_{D=0}^{2047}\|H_D^+\|_F^2}{\frac1{2048}\sum_{D=0}^{2047}\|C_D\|_F^2}=\frac{\text{mean per-distance squared energy of }H^+}{\text{mean per-distance squared energy of }C}.

For example, if these two mean squared energies are 30 and 100, the positive-energy fraction is 30%. We average the squared energies, after computing them at each distance; we do not average the matrices before squaring. Nor do we take an equally weighted average of the per-distance fractions, which can give a different answer when complete-C energy varies with distance. The distance-wise additive identity above ensures fH++fH−+fA=1f_{H^+}+f_{H^-}+f_A=1. Section 2.3 plots the distribution of these path-level fractions. A matrix-energy fraction is distinct from a fraction of an observed attention score or margin.

3 Complete matching scores and positive-spectral geometry

We start from the complete matching score to ask when different token identities can each rank their own representations highest. Token representations vary around their respective centers. The full margin connects this requirement to the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of CC, and then identifies the role of eigenvector directions associated with positive eigenvalues of HH within that same comparison.

Let token ii have a center μi\mu_i in a shared space. Two representations xix_i and xi′x_i' can differ through deviations around that same center; a competing token j≠ij\ne i has its own center μj\mu_j: xi=μi+ϵi,xi′=μi+ϵi′,xj=μj+ϵj.x_i=\mu_i+\epsilon_i,\qquad x_i'=\mu_i+\epsilon_i',\qquad x_j=\mu_j+\epsilon_j. The deviations capture variation across contexts and layers. For a matching score s(x,y)s(x,y), define the pairwise margin mij(s):=s(xi,xi′)−s(xi,xj)=s(μi+ϵi,μi+ϵi′)−s(μi+ϵi,μj+ϵj).\begin{aligned} m_{ij}(s)&:=s(x_i,x_i')-s(x_i,x_j)\\ &=s(\mu_i+\epsilon_i,\mu_i+\epsilon_i') -s(\mu_i+\epsilon_i,\mu_j+\epsilon_j). \end{aligned} Self-matching has the strictly highest score when mij(s)>0m_{ij}(s)>0 for every competing representation under the variations being considered. Modern Hopfield networks address a related separation-and-perturbation problem: comparing a perturbed input with fixed stored patterns . In our setting, the representations on both sides of a score vary across contexts and layers, so we expand the deviations of both same-token and different-token representations.

3.1.0.2 From positive energy to a possible training direction

The energy distributions in Section 2 show that the selected functional paths are shifted toward a larger H+H^+ energy fraction. Applying the margin decomposition to H+H^+ separates its center-dependent contribution into center separation G+G^+ and signed center bias b+b^+; T+T^+ retains the contribution of representation deviations.

Increasing the positive eigenvalue weights along directions that separate token centers increases G+G^+. In this sense, greater H+H^+ energy can support a larger separation contribution, provided that its directions remain aligned with the token-center differences.

The bias behaves differently. Because H+H^+ is symmetric, bji+=−bij+b_{ji}^+=-b_{ij}^+: a center-norm imbalance that favors one token against another disfavors the reverse comparison. With equal weight on the two orders of each token pair, these bias contributions cancel on average. Thus b+b^+ redistributes the advantage between tokens rather than providing a shared positive separation contribution.

This suggests a possible training optimization direction: increase positive spectral weight along token-separating directions, thereby increasing G+G^+ and supporting a broader self-matching advantage. The complete margin still combines this gain with the changes in b+b^+ and T+T^+. The following derivation makes these terms explicit.

3.2.3 Two gaps between a route margin and attention

1. A mean does not tell us how consistently the advantage appears. Each of G,b,T,RG,b,T,R, and mm has its own variance across actual comparisons. From center separation to self-matching

In a particular comparison, if this separation is not offset by the signed bias, contextual, and position-correction terms, it supports a positive margin and hence a self-matching advantage.

The center-separation term depends entirely on the symmetric part of the common reference CC matrix. With δ=μiW−μjW\delta=\mu_i^W-\mu_j^W,

GC=12δ⊤Cref+Cref⊤2δ=G++G−,G+≥0,G−≤0.G^C=\tfrac12\delta^\top\frac{C_{\mathrm{ref}}+C_{\mathrm{ref}}^\top}{2}\delta=G^++G^-,\qquad G^+\geq0,\quad G^-\leq0.

Its positive contribution comes from H+H^+; the negative symmetric component contributes nonpositively, and the antisymmetric component contributes no GG term. This gives a mechanistic interpretation of the high H+H^+ energy observed in functional routes: positive eigenvalue weights can reward separation between token centers. But high H+H^+ energy is not a sufficient condition for a large G+G^+. The positive directions must also align with the relevant token differences.

The rotation experiments below test this distinction while keeping the positive eigenvalues and energy fixed. Changing the directions substantially reduces G+G^+ and is accompanied by decreases in the matching margins and native route-removal Reader scores. These interventions demonstrate that direction at fixed positive energy can change both the separation geometry and induction behavior. Because the rotations also change b,T,Rb,T,R, they do not isolate the entire behavioral change as an effect of G+G^+ alone.

3.2.4 Rotate H⁺ directions while preserving its spectrum

The positive center term depends on both eigenvalues and directions:

HD+=UDΛD+UD⊤,Gik+(D)=12∑aλa+(D)(ua(D)⊤δik)2.H_D^+=U_D\Lambda_D^+U_D^\top,\qquad G^+_{ik}(D)=\tfrac12\sum_a\lambda_a^+(D)\bigl(u_a(D)^\top\delta_{ik}\bigr)^2.

We test this distinction by fitting one fixed orthogonal ambient rotation OO per Writer–Reader route, then applying it across every distance and every evaluation sequence:

HD+′=OHD+O⊤,CD′=HD+′+HD−+AD.H_D^{+\prime}=O H_D^+O^\top,\qquad C_D'=H_D^{+\prime}+H_D^-+A_D.

The full positive spectrum, rank, trace, and squared Frobenius energy are preserved. The original HD−H_D^- and ADA_D are fixed. The constructed frame also keeps the rotated positive support orthogonal to the old negative support, so the new positive component is the actual positive part of the recombined symmetric matrix, rather than merely an added PSD term.

The rotation is fitted on sequence 0 to reduce G+G^+ while penalizing changes in the reference contextual term. The one-percent geometric target applies to that fitting sequence’s center span; it is not a claim that G+G^+ becomes one percent on all 2,084 sequences. We then freeze the rotation and evaluate all 2,084 sequences—150,000 queries and 20,849,616 margins per head. The other 2,083 sequences are not used to optimize the directions, but the reference centers are shared, so this is not an independent-center generalization test.

For behavior, we replace the corresponding positive-operator contribution in native logits and recompute attention over all causal positions. This is an operator-level native-logit intervention, not a claim that an arbitrary rotated operator is realizable by one same-width set of physical Q/K parameters.
